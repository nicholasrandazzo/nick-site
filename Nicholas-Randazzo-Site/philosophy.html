A developing agenda

Bioethics from within a surgical vocation.

Surgery is never only the correction of a mechanism. It asks one person to place a body, a future, and often a family’s hopes in the hands of another. The surgeon’s technical authority therefore carries moral obligations that cannot be captured by competence alone.

I am especially interested in the ethical life of high-stakes intervention: how risk should be communicated, what consent requires when time is short, how clinicians distinguish rescue from overreach, and how responsibility is distributed across teams whose work must be exact without becoming impersonal.

Cardiac surgery gives these questions particular force. Operations on the heart concern survival and function, but also identity, dependence, frailty, rehabilitation, and the character of the life a patient hopes to resume. My ambition is to study these questions as both a clinician and a scholar.

This is a forward-looking program rather than a claim of completed specialization. It grows from prior work in philosophy, law, clinical imaging, biology, and the history of institutions, and it will develop alongside my medical and surgical training.

Questions ahead

Four lines of inquiry.

The governing concern is not abstract interdisciplinarity, but the moral quality of medical action.

I

Surgical responsibility

The ethical demands created by invasive skill, team authority, procedural risk, and the power to act decisively on another person’s body.

II

Consent and time

How urgency, prognosis, asymmetries of knowledge, and the prospect of irreversible change shape meaningful patient authorization.

III

Personhood after intervention

The relation between bodily repair, dependence, enhancement, disability, self-understanding, and the life to which treatment returns a patient.

IV

Clinical character

The habits of attention, restraint, courage, honesty, and accountability required to use medical knowledge well.

A sole-authored manuscript in bioethics is being prepared for anonymous peer review. It considers moral agency, character, and change over time.

The title, target venue, and identifying details are deliberately withheld from this public website while the work enters peer review. Its role here is to mark an active commitment to bioethical scholarship without compromising the conditions under which that scholarship will be assessed.

Archival humanities

Institutions under pressure.

In January 2026, I conducted primary-source research at Stanford University’s Hoover Archives on Vatican governance and ecclesiology during World War II and the early Cold War.

The project examined how theological authority and political pragmatism interacted under ideological and military pressure. That historical work remains relevant to my interest in institutions whose decisions are consequential, constrained, and carried out through complex structures of responsibility.
